# The Night Ferry
The ferry hums across the harbor dark,
its wake a silver seam that will not hold;
a gull rides on the railing like a mark
of punctuation in a sentence, cold.

The passengers are strangers, half asleep,
their faces lit by phones in blue and white;
a child presses her forehead to the deep
and counts the buoys blinking in the night.

We carry what we cannot put to words:
the meeting that went wrong, the unpaid bill,
the voice of someone gone, like distant birds
that call across the water and are still.

And when the engines slow and ropes are thrown,
we step into the city, each alone.

# Inventory of My Father's Garage
Three coffee cans of screws, unsorted.
A calendar from 1994, still on March.
A radio that only gets the ballgame.
Rags that used to be my brother's shirts.

I came to clear it out. I've been here
two hours holding a tape measure,
pulling the yellow tongue out, letting it snap back,
the way he did when he was thinking.

There is a jar of keys to nothing.
There is a level with its bubble
drifted slightly left, which explains
every shelf in the house.

# Villanelle for the Rising Water
The river rises slowly through the town;
we stack the sandbags higher every year.
We tell ourselves the water won't come down.

The church bell rings, the mayor wears a frown,
the radio repeats what we all fear:
the river rises slowly through the town.

The children float their paper boats and drown
them laughing in the puddles by the pier.
We tell ourselves the water won't come down.

The old men argue in the coffee shop, renown
for knowing every flood since they were here.
The river rises slowly through the town.

We pack the photographs, the wedding gown,
the letters that we never meant to clear.
We tell ourselves the water won't come down.

And when at last the levee lets it drown
the streets we loved, we'll stand and hold what's dear.
The river rises slowly through the town.
We tell ourselves the water won't come down.

# What the Beekeeper Knows in January
The hives are wrapped in tar paper,
humming like a refrigerator in another room.
You don't open them. You put your ear
against the wood and listen for the cluster,
that low gold sound of forty thousand bodies
shivering to keep the queen alive.

My grandfather did this every morning,
boots in the snow, coffee steaming,
saying nothing, then saying, good, good,
as if the bees could hear him,
as if the whole house depended on it.

Which it did, in a way. Which it does.

# Ode to a Secondhand Coat
O coat of someone else's winters,
your pockets keep a stranger's lint,
a ticket stub, a peppermint,
a button sewn with thread that splinters.

You've walked through cities I won't see,
been hung on hooks in kitchens warm,
and shielded someone from a storm
before you came at last to me.

I wear you now to catch the bus;
you smell of smoke and cedar chests.
You fit me well, as if you guessed
how cold the world can be to us.

# Elegy for a Dog Named Biscuit
You waited by the door for fourteen years,
your tail a metronome of pure belief
that every car was us. Now no one hears
the clicking of your nails. The house is brief

with silence where you were. Your leash still hangs
beside the coats. Your bowl is in the sink.
I keep forgetting, and the kitchen pangs
each time I drop a crust and start to think

you'll catch it. You were never very wise,
you barked at mailmen, pumpkins, and the wind,
but you looked at us with such forgiving eyes
it felt as if we'd never really sinned.

Sleep under the lilac, Biscuit, sleep.
The yard is yours. The promise is ours to keep.

# Learning English at Forty-Six
In my language, the word for window
also means a small opportunity.
Here it is only glass.

I practice in the car: the, the, the,
my tongue between my teeth like a child
caught stealing. My daughter corrects me
gently, the way I once corrected her
when she said the moon was following us.

At the pharmacy I point. At the bank I nod.
At night I dream in both languages
and wake up homeless in each.

Still, yesterday the cashier laughed at my joke.
It was a small window. I climbed through.

# Ghazal of the Empty Station
The trains don't stop here anymore; the station waits.
The clock above the platform stopped at four; the station waits.

The benches carved with names of lovers long since wed
still face the tracks as if they knew the score; the station waits.

A sparrow nests inside the ticket booth's cracked glass,
and ivy climbs the waiting room's locked door; the station waits.

My grandfather left from here for the war in '43;
he came back thinner, quiet to the core. The station waits.

I come on Sundays, sit, and listen for the whistle,
as if the past could arrive once more. The station waits.

# Sonnet for the Night Nurse
She walks the corridor at half past three,
her shoes so quiet that the sleepers stay
asleep; she checks the charts, the IV's sway,
and smooths a blanket no one else will see.

She knows which patient likes the window cracked,
which one will cry at four and want to talk,
which one has no one coming. In her walk
there is a kind of mercy, plain and exact.

At seven she will drive home through the sun,
eat cereal standing up, and close the blind,
and sleep while all the healthy world goes on,
the faces of the night still in her mind.

We never learn her name. We should. We don't.
She'll come again tonight. Of course she won't

forget us.

# Harvest, Late
The combine sits in the north field like a dinosaur
that died mid-sentence. Too wet to cut.
My father stands at the kitchen window
with his coffee, saying the weather report
back to the television, word for word,
as if repeating it might change it.

The bank called twice. Mom answered once.
The soybeans are fine, he says, they're fine,
another week won't hurt them.
His jaw says something else.

I am nineteen and home for the weekend.
I don't know anything about soybeans.
I stand next to him at the window anyway,
and we both watch it rain.

# Ballad of the Lighthouse Keeper
The keeper climbed the iron stair
each night for forty years,
he lit the lamp with steady hands
and swallowed all his fears.

The ships went by like passing thoughts,
some near and some afar;
he never knew the sailors' names,
but they all knew his star.

One winter came a dreadful gale,
the waves rose up like hills;
he kept the light alight till dawn
with nothing but his will.

They found him sleeping by the lamp,
his coat still wet with spray,
the light still burning through the fog
long after it was day.

# Tercets for My Mother's Hands
Her hands smelled of onions and Jergens lotion,
of the pennies she counted at the kitchen table
the week before payday, lips moving.

Her knuckles got big the way her mother's did.
She'd hold them up and laugh, look at these,
like two bags of walnuts, and keep on peeling.

Now they rest on the hospital sheet, quiet,
the wedding ring loose, turned the wrong way.
I turn it back. She doesn't wake.

I have her hands. I notice it typing,
or holding a cup, or reaching across
a table for someone who isn't there.

# Spoken Word: Overtime
They say hustle like it's a hymn,
like the alarm clock is a prayer bell,
like double shifts are a kind of grace.
My uncle worked thirty years at the plant,
got a plaque, a handshake, a bad back,
and a pension that disappeared the same year as his hair.
He used to say, a job is a job,
and I used to think that was a shrug.
Now I know it was a whole philosophy:
you show up, you do the thing,
you come home and you kiss your kids
with hands that still smell like machine oil,
and you don't let the world tell you
that's not a kind of poetry.
It is. It was. It always was.

Some days I am a front moving in from the west,

over the kitchen and the living room.

# Blank Verse: The Surveyor
He walked the land before the land had lines,
a tripod on his shoulder, chain in hand,
and where he stopped, a county came to be.
The creek became a border, and the hill
a property; the oak tree turned a corner
of someone's farm that someone else would lose.
He never thought of that. He thought of angles,
of how the sun at noon would fix the north,
of how a careful man could pin the world
with numbers to a page and call it known.
And yet at night, beside his little fire,
he'd hear the coyotes singing past his marks,
and wonder who had drawn the lines for them.

# Dramatic Monologue: The Retiring Teacher
You want to know what thirty-four years teaches?
That every September smells like pencil shavings
and floor wax and fear, mine as much as theirs.
That the quiet ones are not always fine.
That the loud ones are almost never fine.
That a boy named Danny Ruiz in 1997
couldn't read until he was twelve, and then
read every book on my shelf, and wrote to me
from Iraq, and then didn't write again.
I keep his letters in my desk. I'm taking those.
The rest, the posters, the stapler, the plants,
you can have them. The new teacher can have them.
Tell her to learn their names in the first week.
Tell her it matters more than she thinks.

# A Letter Never Sent
Dear brother, I've started this letter
eleven times. The first ten
were angry. This one is just tired.

I saw your daughter's picture online.
She has Mom's chin. I didn't know
whether to like it, so I didn't.

I still have your baseball glove.
I don't know why. It doesn't fit me,
and I don't play. It's just there,
in the closet, being yours.

If you called, I would answer.
I want you to know that.
I'm not going to mail this.

# Coffee at Dawn
Before the house wakes up I own the kitchen:
the kettle's rising argument, the blue
flame lapping at the pot, the window glistening
with dew, the neighbor's porch light coming through.

This is the hour I don't belong to anyone,
not to the job, the kids, the phone, the news;
the coffee's bitter and the day's not begun,

a request for cereal, a missing shoe,
and I set down my cup, not by choice,

# Watching My Father Get Old
He asks me to read the menu to him.
He says the print is small. It isn't.
I read it slowly, the way he read to me
Goodnight Moon a thousand times,
and he orders what he always orders,
and I pretend that this is normal.

In the parking lot he hands me the keys
without a word. That's the first time.
I adjust the seat. He looks out the window
at the town he built houses in for forty years,
pointing, that one, and that one, and that one,
as if he is signing his name.

# Hope, After
The doctor said the word remission
like she was handing me a bird,
something small and alive and likely
to fly off if I held it wrong.

I drove home with it in my chest.
I didn't play the radio.
At the light on Grant Street
a man was selling roses from a bucket
and I bought all of them, eleven roses,
and he looked at me like I was crazy,
and I was, a little,
and I said thank you, thank you,
and he said, ma'am, it's just flowers.
It wasn't.
